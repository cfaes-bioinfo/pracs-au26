Generative AI background

AI in biology and omics

While conventional machine learning (ML) is AI that has been around for decades and is commonly used in biology, deep learning, based on large neural networks, has taken off more recently:

1. Predictive deep learning

Predicts an answer for a given input, e.g.:

  • DeepVariant calls DNA variants from sequencing data.
  • AlphaFold 2 predicts 3D protein structure from sequence.

Also used outside of omics/bioinformatics, like image classification, e.g., characterizing soybean defoliation from drone images (Zhang et al. 2022).

2. Generative AI (genAI)

Creates new content such as text, images, code, proteins, and DNA. In biology:

  • RFdiffusion designs new proteins.
  • Evo 2 generates DNA sequences at scale.


Plant pathology example: design of new rice immune receptors that recognize pathogen proteins (Zhu et al. 2026).

AI hierarchy

  • Generative AI is built on deep learning, which is a subset of neural networks, which is a subset of machine learning, which is a subset of artificial intelligence.

    (In the figure, GANs and LLMs are examples of genAI.)

  • Large Language Models (LLMs) are the kind of generative AI that understands and generates text, including code.



Image by Gladstone Institutes

Large Language Models (LLMs)

LLMs have been extremely prominent since ChatGPT’s launch in late 2022.

In simple terms, LLMs such as ChatGPT aim to predict the next part of a word or phrase based on a user-provided text prompt.

— Cooper et al. (2024)

However:

  • Older models only looked at the last few words (e.g., early search autocomplete).

  • LLMs use an architecture called “transformers”, which considers the broader context:
    the full input text and the output generated so far.

What’s in a name: ChatGPT

G = Generative

  • The model creates new content.

P = Pre-trained

  • It only knows about the data it was trained on.
  • Anything after the training cutoff date is unknown to the model.

T = Transformer

  • The architecture that lets it consider the broader context of the text.

Beyond the training data

Many genAI tools can also use information outside of their training data, such as a file you upload or web search results, by adding it to the input text.

This has become much more common in the last few years: many tools now automatically search the web when a question is about recent events.

How LLMs are trained

1. Pre-training

  • Learn to predict the next word from huge amounts of text (web, books, code).
  • The model knows a lot, but can’t yet answer questions or follow instructions.

2. Post-training

  • Train on example conversations.
  • “Reinforcement learning”: reward good answers, as rated by humans or checked automatically.
  • The model now follows instructions and acts as an assistant.


Why this matters

  • Human ratings reward answers people like, which is one cause of LLM sycophancy (Sharma et al. 2023).
  • Automatic checks work especially well for code: one reason why LLMs are particularly strong at coding.

Tokens and the context window

Tokens

  • LLMs read and write tokens, not words: pieces of words, roughly ¾ of a word each.
  • Usage limits and costs are counted in tokens.

Context window

  • The maximum number of tokens a model can consider at once: its “working memory”.
  • Includes everything in the conversation: all your prompts, uploaded files, and the model’s responses.

Performance degrades with conversation length

Answers get worse well before the context window is full: e.g., information in the middle of a long conversation tends to get “lost”. Once it is full, older parts are dropped or summarized.

Start a new chat for each new task — and even within a task, once a conversation gets long.

Making sense of model names

A model name like Claude Sonnet 5.5 combines three “levels”:

1. Company

Each company makes its own models. The three at the frontier:

  • Anthropic: Claude
  • OpenAI: GPT
  • Google: Gemini

2. Tier

Companies offer different models — for Claude:

  • Haiku: small, fast, cheap
  • Sonnet: mid-size
  • Opus: very capable,
    but slower and pricier
  • Fable: top tier, very pricey

3. Version

Each tier is updated regularly:

  • E.g., Claude Opus in 2026: 4.6 → 4.7 → 4.8 → 5 → 5.5.

  • Each update is generally an improvement.

Tool ≠ model: chat tools often let you pick a model, and some tools use another company’s models.
For example, the tool Microsoft Copilot mainly runs OpenAI’s GPT models.

A fast-moving field

  • Striking failures like those on the previous slide (from 2025) are often fixed in the next version.
    So, any impressions of what genAI can’t do may quickly become outdated!
  • You should also consider the so-called “jagged frontier” of genAI:
    you can’t generalize its performance or intelligence based on very bad or very good examples.
  • Not all problems have seen quick resolution: e.g., that of making things up (“hallucinations”).

Still, why couldn’t it count letters?

  • What LLMs actually see are tokens converted to numbers, so the individual letters are invisible to them.

  • To see how text is split into tokens, paste “unconventional” and a DNA sequence like ATGGCGTTAGCCATTAGC into a tokenizer . Click “Token IDs” to see the numbers the model receives.

  • As applied to omics: don’t ask a chat AI to count bases or reverse-complement a sequence by reading it. Ask it for code that does this, or to run non-AI tools that do it.

  • But you must always be able to explain your reasoning and code, and document how you used genAI.

In your research

  • Many journals require you to disclose genAI use, and genAI can’t be an author.

  • GenAI output is not reproducible: the same prompt can give a different answer next time, and models are updated and retired.

  • So save the output you actually use: saving only the prompt is not enough to get it back. (And for disclosure, note which tool you used and for what.)

Using genAI inside your IDE

For coding tasks, it’s highly beneficial to use genAI directly inside your IDE, like VS Code:


Modes of interaction

  • Code completions
    Suggestions in the editor as you type.
  • Chat with “agentic” capabilities
    The model can perform actions like editing files and running commands.

Benefits

  • Automatic awareness of your files.
  • Less copy-pasting in both directions (e.g., you can simply “accept” suggestions).
  • An agent can try its own suggestions and fix its own mistakes in a feedback loop.
  • Persistent memory across sessions, e.g., via instruction files saved in your project.

AI agents are convenient and powerful, but that also makes them potentially dangerous.

Running LLMs locally

Cloud-based

  • All the options so far are cloud-based.

  • Each request goes to the provider’s servers, where the models are hosted and run.

  • The models we’ve discussed are proprietary and not available for download.

Local

  • Open-weight models like DeepSeek and Llama can be downloaded and run locally.

  • Small models can run on personal computers; OSC can run larger ones (on GPU nodes).

  • Beyond compute costs, usage (“inference”) is free.

  • Less capable than frontier proprietary models, but not necessarily by much.

We may explore local models at OSC later in the course. OSC is working on setting up a permanently running model, and may offer us some test usage very soon. See osc.edu/AI for current options.

Applications in omics data analysis

“AI in research” can mean very different things

  1. Specialized non-generative models (most machine learning / deep learning)
    Doing a specific task — e.g., AlphaFold 2 and DeepVariant from earlier.

  2. Biological generative models
    Creating new DNA or proteins rather than text — e.g., RFdiffusion and Evo 2 from earlier.

  1. LLMs that run other tools
    An agent that runs other programs, which may or may not use AI themselves. E.g., Claude designing protein binders by running RFdiffusion and similar programs (Claude Science and Shanehsazzadeh 2026).

  2. LLMs doing (most of) the work themselves
    E.g., extracting data from papers, interpreting results like a list of significant genes. Or Claude finding a new family of enzymes by spotting unusual repeats in phage DNA (Yoon et al. 2026).

  1. LLMs helping you write and run code
    The focus of this course.

What should you (not) use genAI for?

Good uses

  • Tasks where you can check the result without excessive effort.
  • Tedious tasks you know how to do: documentation, reformatting, repetitive work.
  • Learning, when you use it as a tutor (explain, give examples, quiz me).

Be careful with

  • Tasks where you can’t judge the output.
  • Numbers and conclusions that the AI reports directly, rather than code you can rerun.
  • Replacing your own learning, especially when building new skills (Lenharo 2026).
  • Sensitive data without proper protection/approval.

Standard chat

  • Answers within seconds (Flash) or perhaps a minute (Thinking).
  • Good for coding-related questions and many other fairly specific questions.

Deep Research

  • Works for several minutes, searching and reading sources, then producing a report with citations.
  • Good for literature over-
    views and comprehensive tool/method comparisons.

Notebooks

  • You collect sources for a project in one place.
  • Its answers are based on those sources, so you don’t have to re-upload or re-explain.

Tips and pitfalls

Writing effective prompts

General advice

  • Break bigger problems down into smaller ones.
  • When you know what you want, be specific and detailed, e.g., about formats and programs
    (but sometimes you want to explore your options and be less specific).

For certain larger or more complex tasks, consider including details on:

  1. Context — Who you are (or “who the AI is supposed to be”) and what you’re working on
  2. Task — What specifically you want the AI to do
  3. Format — How you want the output structured
  4. Constraints — Any limitations, requirements, or things to avoid
  5. Examples — Sample inputs/outputs

Building up a prompt

Task:

Write a Bash script that counts the number of reads in a FASTQ file.

+ Context & constraints:

[…] The file is gzip-compressed, and its name should be an argument to the script. Only use standard Unix tools.

+ Format & example:

[…] Print file name and read count separated by a tab: sampleA_R1.fastq.gz 250000

Many AI answers are poor because the AI doesn’t know your file names, column names, or software.
AI inside your IDE can see these, which is a big advantage.

“Prompt enhancer” phrases

Explanation

  • Explain with examples
  • Explain as if I’m a high school student

Clarification

To make it less likely that the model makes stuff up:

  • Ask me clarifying questions
  • If you’re not sure about something, say so rather than guessing

Verification

  • Show me the specific quotes from the papers that support each of these conclusions
  • Which parts of this are you least sure about?
  • How can I check that this code does what I want?

Pitfalls I

Hallucinations

Models make things up, e.g.:

  • Invented commands, options, functions, or data columns.
  • Example code and its output — unless the AI actually ran the code, that output is just a prediction.

“Jagged frontier”

  • Models are very good at some tasks but fail surprisingly at others.

  • The frontier keeps moving, so past experience doesn’t tell you which tasks will fail.

“0-to-90% problem”

  • GenAI often gets you most of the way, but not all the way.

  • Finishing the task takes expertise — which is harder when you didn’t do the first 90% yourself.

Pitfalls II

Figure by Hannah Toth

  • Comfort vs. limitation zone
    • Models do well with common knowledge (e.g., grep; referenced millions of times in training data)
    • But hallucinate more with niche or new topics, especially when no clear documentation can be found with an online search.
  • Confidence ≠ correctness
    Models sound equally sure when making things up.
  • Sycophancy
    Models tend to tell you what you want to hear.
    Ask “What could be wrong with this script?” rather than
    “I think this is correct, do you agree?”.

Pitfalls III: “Narrative-flipping”

What is narrative-flipping?

  • When challenged on an answer, like a narrative explaining a set of results, a model may flip to a completely different narrative.

  • Maddeningly, evidence previously interpreted to support one answer may be re-interpreted to support a completely different answer.

  • Causes include sycophancy and premature commitment due to how text is generated one token at a time. Once it goes in a certain direction, its bias is to justify that further (Zhang et al. 2023).

To avoid narrative-flipping

  • Facts before conclusions: first ask only for the evidence and arguments on all sides.

  • Competing explanations: have the model build several mutually exclusive explanations and weigh the evidence for each before picking one.

  • Pre-empt caving: tell the model not to change its answer when challenged, unless you provide new facts.

  • Second opinion: start a new chat or use another model, rather than pushing back in the same chat.
