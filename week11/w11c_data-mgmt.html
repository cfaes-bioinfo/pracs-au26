Raw data management

Any type of raw data from your research is extremely valuable: these are the results of your experiment, and they may have been labor-intensive and expensive to produce. With omics projects, your main raw data will usually be a set of FASTQ files, but similar principles apply to other types of data.

  • Never delete your raw data. Even after you “finished” your analyses or produced processed files that you find more useful, your ground truth will always be the raw data: it allows you and others to reproduce your results, and to re-analyze the data when e.g. new methods become available.
  • Keep multiple copies of your raw data, ideally in at least two different physical locations. Even if your data mainly lives on OSC, also keep a copy elsewhere — and at OSC, never only store files in a scratch dir, where files that haven’t been used for a while are automatically deleted.
  • Share your raw data publicly after publication. Most journals and funding agencies nowadays require this, and sequencing data has long been expected to be shared publicly.

Raw data management: other recommendations

  • Keep track of data provenance and metadata:
    • When and from where was the data obtained/received?
    • What metadata exists for each sample?
  • Use standard and preferably plain-text file formats.
  • To avoid accidents, consider making your data read-only with file permissions (bonus material).
  • After downloading or when sharing key data, consider using “checksums” to check that files were transferred entirely (bonus material).

Metadata in spreadsheets

Sample metadata, such as which treatment and time point each sample belongs to, is often entered in a spreadsheet program like Excel. That is fine, as long as you organize the spreadsheet so that it can be read by code without manual cleanup. The Turing Way’s Data Organisation in Spreadsheets page has detailed recommendations, including:

  • Use one row per sample (or observation) and one column per variable, with a single header row at the top.
  • Put only one value in each cell.
  • Don’t use merged cells, colors, or other formatting to store information: if you want to highlight some cells, add a column for this instead.
  • Use consistent sample names, category labels, and units throughout.
  • Avoid spaces and special characters in sample names and column names (e.g. use time_point rather than Time point (days)).
  • Write dates as YYYY-MM-DD (e.g. 2026-11-04), which is unambiguous and sorts correctly.
  • Choose a single way to indicate missing data (e.g. NA), rather than leaving some cells empty.
  • Don’t do calculations or analyses in the spreadsheet that holds your raw metadata.
  • Save (export) the final version as a plain-text CSV or TSV file, which is what you’ll read into your analysis code.

Metadata in spreadsheets (cont.)

For an example, take a look at the garrigos/meta/metadata.tsv file you’ve seen before, with one row per sample and one column each for the sample ID, time point, and treatment.

In week 13, you’ll see that this kind of layout is called “tidy data”, and that R works with it very well.


Excel turns some gene names into dates

Excel automatically converts text that looks like a date or number. For example, the gene names SEPT2 and MARCH1 become 2-Sep and 1-Mar. This is so common that around 20% of published papers with gene lists in Excel files contained such errors, and several human genes were renamed to avoid the problem (e.g. SEPT2 is now SEPTIN2). To prevent this, set such columns to the “Text” format before entering or pasting data.

Sharing data and other files

This policy is now in effect:

By the end of 2025, all federally-funded research must make articles and data publicly available immediately upon journal publication with no waiting periods allowed.

— Federal Research Policy Guide from OSU’s Enterprise for Research, Innovation and Knowledge

Data Management Plans

Data Management Plans (DMPs), which specify how you are planning to manage and share your data, are also required by major funding agencies (e.g., NSF has required them since 2011):

A DMP is currently required for all competitive grant programs of the United States Department of Agriculture and the National Science Foundation.

— Grünwald et al. (2024)

Here are specific guidelines for USDA NIFA and NSF grants.

Platforms for sharing data

  • Raw sequences from high-throughput sequencing, such as FASTQ files, are usually uploaded to NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (or its European counterpart, ENA).
  • Some results should also be uploaded to NCBI, such as genome assemblies and annotations, and gene expression count tables (Gene Expression Omnibus, GEO). For RNA-seq data, a GEO submission also takes care of uploading the reads to the SRA.
  • Other results and data can be shared via general data repositories like Dryad and Zenodo, which journals increasingly require upon publication.
  • Code can be shared via GitHub (or similar platforms like GitLab and Bitbucket), ideally with a permanent Digital Object Identifier (DOI) via Zenodo1.

For more and OSU-specific information about data management and sharing, OSU’s Research Commons has guides and offers consultations.

  1. Some journals require code to be shared via a general repository like Dryad as well, where the code (and especially its documentation) may be subject to manual quality control.

Getting a DOI for your GitHub repository with Zenodo

A GitHub repository can be changed or deleted at any time, which makes it a poor permanent reference in a paper. Zenodo, a general data repository run by CERN, can archive a snapshot of your repo and give it a citable DOI:

  1. Log in to Zenodo with your GitHub account.
  2. In Zenodo, go to your account’s GitHub settings, and flip the switch next to the repository you want to archive (the repository needs to be public).
  3. On GitHub, create a “release” of your repository (on the repository’s front page, click “Create a new release” in the right sidebar).

Zenodo will then automatically archive this release and assign it a DOI, which you can cite in your paper and add as a badge to your README. Each new release gets its own DOI, plus there is a “concept DOI” that always points to the latest version.

See GitHub’s documentation for more details.

Should I keep my OSC and local dirs synced?

You can’t keep files synced between OSC and your local computer in a fully automatic way like with OneDrive or Dropbox. And because the amount of data on OSC is typically very large, you may not want to either. Instead, here is a good strategy:

  • Keep the files you have under version control (code, etc.) synced via Git/GitHub.
  • Periodically sync all or a selection of other files with the data transfer methods discussed next.

Data syncing is especially important when you do analyses both at OSC and on your local computer for a given project. To reduce this friction, I recommend that for projects for which you need OSC, you instead try to do all your work there.

When you do that, it even works to keep entirely separate sets of files at OSC and your local computer – for example, you may keep only the manuscript and related files on your local computer (and/or OneDrive/Teams), and everything else at OSC.
